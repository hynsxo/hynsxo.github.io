Robotics · Embedded SystemsDec 2024 – Feb 2025
Undergraduate Intern, AI Research Group, Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST)
10-DOF Dual-Arm Drumming Robot
C/C++CANopenGDB

I developed a 10-DOF dual-arm robot that reads a score and plays the drums. At every strike, though, the arm movement was slightly off and looked unnatural.

  • Tracing the CANopen communication data and the motor-control flow, I found that at the moment a motor switched between position control and torque control, it was using not its latest position but the value from the previous 5 ms communication cycle.
  • One suggestion was to shorten the communication cycle from 5 ms to 1 ms. But the whole motion pipeline, which picks the next strike position from the score and computes motor targets with inverse kinematics (IK), was built around the 5 ms cycle, so changing it would have meant rewriting and re-validating the motion calculation as well.
  • So I studied the motor protocol documentation and focused on SDO, a request–response message that can read a value from a specific motor. I kept the existing cyclic PDO communication, which was working correctly, and designed and implemented a hybrid structure that reads the latest position over SDO only at the moment of a mode switch. Because SDO carries more overhead than PDO, I limited it to those moments.
  • A motion that was fine in simulation once collided on the real robot and broke an expensive part. I confirmed that the simulation did not reflect joint wear or changes in inertia, and created a three-step procedure for every new motion: verify in simulation, run on the real robot at 50% speed, then record the error and add a safety margin to the trajectory.
  • I also generated trajectories that reduce torso movement using Dijkstra’s algorithm and interpolation, tuned the motor PID parameters, and debugged with GDB.
  • I wrote up GitHub collaboration conventions and a diagram of the code flow for each performance state, and shared them with the team.

The striking motion became smooth while changes to the existing control structure were kept to a minimum. After the three-step procedure was introduced there was not a single collision, and the team adopted it as its standard procedure.

Data AnalyticsApr 2024 – Aug 2024
Land & Transportation Data Utilization Competition
Team UAIN · Korea Airports Corporation President’s Award (Excellence Prize)
PythonOpenCVQGISRegression Analysis

Urban air mobility (UAM) is forecast to operate more than 200 routes after 2035, but at the time, demonstration projects were flown only along the Han River or over open land far from the city center, so there was no way to check noise impact when designing routes over the city. Our two-person team built a noise-analysis model for this, and I was responsible for calculating each area’s noise-sensitivity weight.

  • Using metropolitan transport-demand data, we chose three sites in central Seoul where UAM demand is expected to concentrate: Gangnam, Hongik University Station, and Seoul Station.
  • We processed building-use data in QGIS to map the density of residential and commercial zones at each site. I built the algorithm that calculates each area’s sensitivity weight, based on the ratio between the residential and commercial noise limits in Korea’s Noise and Vibration Control Act.
  • My teammate handled noise prediction, building regression models from noise measured at Gimpo Airport and from the noise limits in the UAM certification standards, then combining them to estimate noise by distance.
  • When we tried to combine our results, they did not fit together and we disagreed. I suggested restating our shared goal in one sentence, “propose the route that causes the least noise damage to residential areas,” and drew each of our models as a diagram, side by side. Seen that way, the model missing an axis was mine: my teammate’s noise data varied with altitude, while my model gave each area a single weight.
  • So I split the weights by altitude, so that the same residential area becomes more sensitive the lower the aircraft passes. My teammate recalculated the noise data, originally based on conventional aircraft, for UAM vehicles.
  • We set four route directions around each vertiport and compared them cell by cell, entering the predicted noise and the weights into a 17-cell grid (8 cells on each side of the route).

We showed in numbers that the result changes with route direction even at the same site. At Seoul Station, the share of area with an above-average noise weight was 39.7% for the east–west route and 31.8% for the southwest–northeast route. The model received the Korea Airports Corporation President’s Award (Excellence Prize).

AI · BackendMar 2026 – Jun 2026
FollowMe - Diary-Based Tomorrow Prediction Service
Capstone Team Project · Backend and RAG Design
FastAPIAWS EC2FAISSMiniLMGPT API

Keeping a diary every day for eight years taught me that a plan fails easily when it does not reflect how I felt the day before and on the day itself. So for our capstone project, three of us built a service that predicts a user’s next day from their past diary entries and suggests missions to act on. It consists of a Flutter app and a FastAPI server, and I designed the server and the retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline.

  • At first I tried to find “similar days” using structured personality scores. But the patterns people repeat are not in numbers; they are in sentences that carry emotion and context. So I redesigned the system to store diary entries as vectors in advance and, every midnight, retrieve the past entries most similar to that day’s entry as evidence for the prediction.
  • I implemented the search with L2 distance in FAISS, and chose paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 (384 dimensions), a lightweight multilingual embedding model that runs on an EC2 server without a GPU and supports semantic search in Korean.
  • We considered doing everything on the device, but dropped the idea because of response quality and memory limits. Instead, the app handles only input and display, and the server handles embedding, search, and GPT API calls.
  • Because the service handles personal diaries, sensitive information such as phone numbers and resident registration numbers is masked in advance with regular expressions.
  • The service analyzes the gap between a user’s current personality scores and the “ideal self” they set, and suggests four action missions that fit into the predicted day. Completing a mission updates the scores immediately, and the update feeds into the next prediction.

We released the service to thirty students in the same course, who used it for a month, and based on their feedback we added the next day’s weather as an input. I learned that a service is not finished when development ends; it has to keep being improved from how it is actually used.
